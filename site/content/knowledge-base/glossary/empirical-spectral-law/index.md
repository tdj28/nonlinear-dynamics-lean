An **empirical spectral law** is the probability distribution of the
{{< refterm "empirical-spectral-measure" "empirical spectral measure" >}}
produced by a random matrix. One matrix sample gives one measure on the real
line. Repeating the random-matrix experiment gives a distribution over those
measure-valued outcomes. That second distribution is the empirical spectral
law.

This creates three different levels:

1. a sampled Hermitian matrix \(H\);
2. its sample empirical spectral measure \(L_H\), which is a measure on
   \(\mathbb R\); and
3. the law \(\mathcal Q_n\) of \(L_H\), which is a measure on a space whose
   points are themselves measures.

A fourth object, the mean empirical spectral measure, averages the
measure-valued outcomes. It is not the same object as their law.

## Precise definition

Fix a matrix dimension \(n\). Let \(\mathbb P_n\) be a probability law on
intrinsic \(n\)-by-\(n\) Hermitian matrices, and let

\[
L_n:H\longmapsto L_H
\]

be the measurable map sending one matrix to its empirical spectral measure.
The empirical spectral law is the pushforward

\[
\mathcal Q_n=(L_n)_\#\mathbb P_n.
\]

For a measurable collection \(C\) of measures,

\[
\mathcal Q_n(C)
{} =
\mathbb P_n\bigl(\{H:L_H\in C\}\bigr).
\]

The notation \((L_n)_\#\mathbb P_n\) means that probability mass is transported
through the observable \(L_n\). Measurability is indispensable: without it,
the preimage used on the right need not be a measurable event.

In the checked Gaussian unitary ensemble (GUE) layer,
\(\mathbb P_n\) is <code>GUE.intrinsicLaw n</code> and the law is defined by

~~~lean
noncomputable def GUE.empiricalSpectralLaw (n : ℕ) :
    Measure (Measure ℝ) :=
  (GUE.intrinsicLaw n).map RandomMatrix.empiricalSpectralMeasure
~~~

The nested type <code>Measure (Measure ℝ)</code> is the key. The outer measure
describes randomness across matrix samples. Each point in its sample space is
an inner measure on \(\mathbb R\).

Mathlib equips <code>Measure ℝ</code> with the Giry measurable structure,
generated by evaluation maps on measurable sets. It proves that pushforward,
Dirac embedding, and monadic join are measurable operations in this setting
([Mathlib contributors](#ref-esl-mathlib-giry)). The project uses that official
interface rather than inventing a separate measurable space of finite
spectra.

## Four objects that must not collapse

| Object | Lean-shaped type | What varies? | Mass statement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matrix sample \(H\) | <code>HermitianEuclidean n</code> | Matrix entries | Not itself a measure |
| Sample empirical measure \(L_H\) | <code>Measure ℝ</code> | Eigenvalue locations of one matrix | Mass one for \(n\gt0\); zero at \(n=0\) |
| Empirical spectral law \(\mathcal Q_n\) | <code>Measure (Measure ℝ)</code> | Which measure-valued sample occurred | Mass one in every dimension |
| Mean empirical measure \(\overline L_n\) | <code>Measure ℝ</code> | No sample remains after averaging | Mass one for \(n\gt0\); zero at \(n=0\) |

The law and the mean happen to live one level apart:
\(\mathcal Q_n\) is a measure **on measures**, while \(\overline L_n\) is one
measure on the spectral line. Knowing only the mean generally loses
sample-to-sample variation.

## A toy two-sample audit

This example is deliberately not a GUE calculation. It exists only to make the
type levels concrete.

Suppose a random source chooses either

\[
H_+=
\begin{bmatrix}
2&0\\
0&0
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
H_-=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
0&-2
\end{bmatrix}
\]

with equal probability. The two sample empirical measures are

\[
L_+=\frac12\delta_2+\frac12\delta_0,
\qquad
L_-=\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\delta_{-2}.
\]

The empirical spectral law is

\[
\mathcal Q
{} =
\frac12\delta_{L_+}+\frac12\delta_{L_-}.
\]

Its atoms are \(L_+\) and \(L_-\), not the real numbers \(2,0,-2\). The mean
empirical measure is instead

\[
\overline L
{} =
\frac14\delta_2+\frac12\delta_0+\frac14\delta_{-2}.
\]

The law remembers that an entire two-atom measure arrived on each trial. The
mean remembers only the averaged spectral mass. For example, the two sample
first moments are \(1\) and \(-1\), while their average is zero. The two sample
second moments are both \(2\). None of these toy values is a finite-GUE
normalization claim.

## Raw measure-valued law versus probability-valued law

In positive dimension, every sample empirical spectral measure has total mass
one. The project therefore provides a bundled
<code>ProbabilityMeasure ℝ</code> version of each sample:

~~~lean
RandomMatrix.empiricalSpectralProbability (n : ℕ) :
    HermitianEuclidean (n + 1) → ProbabilityMeasure ℝ
~~~

Pushing the intrinsic GUE law through this map gives

~~~lean
GUE.empiricalSpectralProbabilityLaw (n : ℕ) :
    ProbabilityMeasure (ProbabilityMeasure ℝ)
~~~

The parameter \(n\) here describes matrix dimension \(n+1\). Positivity is
built into that successor form.

The project also packages the raw law itself:

~~~lean
GUE.empiricalSpectralLawProbability (n : ℕ) :
    ProbabilityMeasure (Measure ℝ)
~~~

Mathlib's bundled probability-measure subtype carries the mass-one proof while
coercing back to its underlying raw measure
([Mathlib contributors](#ref-esl-mathlib-probability)).

These similarly named objects answer different questions:

- <code>empiricalSpectralLawProbability n</code> says the **outer law** has
  total mass one. Its outcomes remain raw <code>Measure ℝ</code> values.
- <code>empiricalSpectralProbabilityLaw n</code> says that, in matrix
  dimension \(n+1\), each **inner outcome** is bundled as a probability
  measure and the outer object is also a probability measure.

The theorem <code>map_empiricalSpectralProbabilityLaw_coe</code> proves that
forgetting the inner probability wrapper recovers
<code>empiricalSpectralLaw (n + 1)</code>. Packaging changes the type-level
evidence, not the underlying spectral masses.

## Dimension zero: a probability law at the zero measure

The empty spectrum forces a careful distinction between an outcome and the law
of that outcome.

The sample empirical measure is defined from the spectral counting measure by
extended-nonnegative scaling:

\[
L_H=(n:\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\infty})^{-1}N_H.
\]

At \(n=0\), the inverse in \(\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\infty}\) is infinity, while the
counting measure \(N_H\) is zero because its index type is empty. Mathlib's
scalar action gives infinity times the zero measure as the zero measure.
Therefore \(L_H=0\).

That zero measure is not a probability measure. Yet the random matrix is
deterministically the unique empty Hermitian matrix, so the **law** of its
empirical measure is a genuine probability law:

\[
\mathcal Q_0=\delta_0.
\]

Here the outer Dirac measure has total mass one and is concentrated at the
inner zero measure. The checked theorem is
<code>GUE.empiricalSpectralLaw_zero</code>.

This is why the raw law exists in every dimension while the
probability-measure-valued law is exposed only in positive dimension. A
probability distribution may be concentrated at an object that is not itself
a probability measure.

## Normalized spectral moments

For one Hermitian sample, the \(k\)-th complex empirical spectral moment is

\[
m_k(H)
{} =
\int_{\mathbb R}(x:\mathbb C)^k\,\mathrm dL_H(x).
\]

The first two sample identities are

\[
m_1(H)
{} =
\left(\frac1n:\mathbb C\right)\operatorname{Tr}(H),
\qquad
m_2(H)
{} =
\left(\frac1n:\mathbb C\right)\operatorname{Tr}(H^2).
\]

The checked Lean theorems hold for every natural \(n\), including zero. The
coefficient on the right is the real field inverse embedded into
\(\mathbb C\). In a field,

\[
(0:\mathbb R)^{-1}=0.
\]

This is not the same intermediate arithmetic as the extended-nonnegative
inverse used to define \(L_H\). The all-dimensional moment identity is correct
because conversion to a real scalar sends the infinite extended value to zero,
and the empty trace is also zero.

Under the project's Wigner-scaled finite GUE law,

\[
\mathbb E[m_1(H)]=0,
\qquad
\mathbb E[m_2(H)]
{} =
\left(\frac1n:\mathbb C\right)n.
\]

Thus the second expectation is zero at \(n=0\) and one for every \(n\gt0\).
The positive-dimensional corollary is
<code>GUE.integral_empiricalSpectralMoment_two_succ</code>.

These are expectations of the sample moment observable under the intrinsic
matrix law. They are not a joint eigenvalue density and do not imply a
semicircle limit. Two finite moments cannot determine the full law.

## The mean measure is a Giry join

Given a law \(\mathcal Q_n\) on measures, Mathlib's
<code>Measure.join</code> integrates the inner measures set by set. The project
defines

\[
\overline L_n=\operatorname{join}(\mathcal Q_n).
\]

This flattening operation is the measure-level multiplication associated with
the probability monad introduced by Giry ([Giry](#ref-esl-giry)); exact claims
here use Mathlib's checked interface.

For a measurable set \(B\), the Giry construction has the interpretation

\[
\overline L_n(B)
{} =
\int_{\operatorname{Measure}(\mathbb R)}\mu(B)\,
  \mathrm d\mathcal Q_n(\mu).
\]

The checked module proves that \(\overline L_0=0\) and that
\(\overline L_{n+1}\) is a probability measure. It does **not** prove the
Bochner-integral interchange

\[
\int_{\mathbb R}(x:\mathbb C)^k\,\mathrm d\overline L_n(x)
\stackrel{?}{=}
\mathbb E[m_k(H)].
\]

That equality is mathematically natural under appropriate measurability and
integrability conditions, but it is not part of this formalized slice. Do not
cite the sample expectation theorems as if a moment theorem for the Giry join
had been checked.

## Intrinsic and ambient presentations

The finite GUE law exists in two compatible forms:

- <code>GUE.intrinsicLaw n</code> lives directly on intrinsic Hermitian
  matrices;
- <code>GUE.matrixLaw n</code> lives on all complex square matrices and gives
  the Hermitian locus full mass.

The ambient empirical-spectral observable first maps a Hermitian matrix into
the intrinsic carrier and sends a non-Hermitian input to zero. RMT-10B proved
that observable measurable. RMT-10C proves

\[
\mathcal Q_n =
(\operatorname{ambientEmpiricalSpectralMeasure})_\#
  \operatorname{matrixLaw}_n.
\]

The non-Hermitian fallback is irrelevant almost everywhere under finite GUE
because the ambient law is Hermitian with probability one. This equality is a
transport theorem, not a spectral theory for non-Hermitian matrices.

## What the term does not imply

An empirical spectral law does not by itself provide:

- a formula for the joint eigenvalue density;
- absolute continuity of the mean empirical measure;
- a Vandermonde determinant;
- independent eigenvalues;
- a semicircle law;
- convergence as matrix dimension grows;
- concentration, rigidity, or extreme-eigenvalue statistics;
- a spacing or unfolding convention; or
- a theorem interchanging mean-measure moments with expected sample moments.

The checked RMT-10C layer proves a finite pushforward law, its probability
packaging, its empty-dimensional boundary, its mean measure, and the first two
expected sample moments. Each later analytic claim needs its own hypotheses
and formal proof. The standard random-matrix literature places these finite
objects within a much larger spectral and asymptotic program
([Anderson, Guionnet, and Zeitouni](#ref-esl-agz)).

## Exercises

1. In the toy two-sample model, evaluate \(\mathcal Q(\{L_+\})\) and
   \(\overline L(\{0\})\). Explain why these numbers live at different type
   levels.
2. Construct two different laws on probability measures that have the same
   mean measure. What information does the mean discard?
3. Explain why \(\delta_0\), as a law on <code>Measure ℝ</code>, is a
   probability measure even though its unique outcome \(0\) is not.
4. For \(n\gt0\), derive \(m_1(H)\) and \(m_2(H)\) from the trace and
   trace-square identities for the spectral counting measure.
5. Audit the two inverses at dimension zero. Which inverse is infinity, which
   inverse is zero, and at what stage does each appear?
6. Draw the measurable pushforward square connecting intrinsic matrices,
   ambient matrices, and raw empirical spectral measures.
7. State the extra theorem needed before replacing an expected sample moment
   by a moment of the mean empirical measure.
